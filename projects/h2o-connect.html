The problem

Water comes for a few hours. Nobody can see the tank.

Piped water arrives for a few hours a day. Homes store it in a rooftop tank and pump it up by hand, with no gauge and no warning.

Every overflow is water the household already paid to pump.

of rural homes now have a tap. The problem has moved indoors.

the gap

People check the tank because nothing proves what it is doing. The product has to supply the proof.

how I worked

Desk research into piped supply and the float switches and app controllers on sale. No formal interviews or usability study: the design changes in the stories below came from my own reviews.

The kit and app were then installed and tested with Dwaj‑Tech in about 100 homes in Visakhapatnam, Narsapuram and Hyderabad. After that I reviewed my own screens and rebuilt them (decision 5).

Decision 1 · strategic · weeks 1 and 2

Why I designed an app people should open less

Most apps want a daily open. This one works when the person who runs the pump stops climbing up to check the tank.

before

A remote control for the motor

The float switches and app controllers on sale could start and stop a pump. None could say what the tank was about to do, or prove what it had just done. So people kept checking by hand: listening for the overflow, climbing to the roof, switching off early.

options I weighed

A remote for the motor
What the products on sale already do. It saves the walk to the switch, not the climb to the roof.
A fully automatic black box
Nothing to learn and nothing to trust. The first silent failure sends people back to checking.
A witness that can act
Chosen. It shows what it is about to do, proves what it did, and stops in one tap.

decision

Not a controller. A witness that can act.

State and evidence come first, controls second. Every automatic action leaves a receipt, and one tap takes control back. Four principles hold every screen to that.

trade-off

More to show, less magic

Every automatic action now needs evidence and a receipt on screen. That costs space and design time a plain remote would not.

State before controls

rules out: a home screen that opens on a power button

Every action leaves a receipt

rules out: silent schedules and automation with no history

Colour means something

rules out: colour used only to fill space

The wall switch always wins

rules out: any flow that traps a household inside the app

Three visual directions were scored against the principles. None held all four, so the final design keeps one thing from each.

After: the tank first, the controls second.

result

In about 100 homes, with the app

Installed and tested with Dwaj‑Tech. The target is fewer than three app opens a week by week six, which would mean people have stopped checking. It has not been measured yet.

Decision 2 · strategic · weeks 3 and 4

Why Stop never asks “Are you sure?”

Stop is the button people reach for when something already looks wrong. A confirmation dialog there costs seconds, and trust.

before

Stop sat below the fold

In my own review of the first layout, Stop needed a scroll on small phones, and it followed the usual pattern of confirming a destructive action. For a motor that may be running dry, both are a delay.

options I weighed

Confirm before stopping
The standard for destructive actions. It costs seconds when the motor may be running dry.
Stop, then offer undo
Kinder to mis-taps, but restarting a pump is not a neutral undo, and the bar covers the reading.
One tap, no dialog, and the wall switch wins
Chosen. A mis-tap costs one restart; a slow stop can cost a motor.

decision

One tap, no dialog, and the wall switch always wins.

Stop sits in the motor card on Home and runs full width on the Motor screen, labelled, in one style. The relay answers in under 200 ms, the app says “Manual until you switch back”, and the Manual switch on the unit overrides the app in hardware.

trade-off

Accidental stops can happen

A stray tap stops the motor and switches to Manual. I accepted that: restarting is one tap, and the app says why it stopped.

Decision 3 · micro · week 4

Why the leak alert proves before it asks

An alert that asks “Close the valve?” before it shows why gets dismissed, or believed without a reason.

before

The question came first

My first version led with the two buttons and put the evidence underneath. Reviewing it, I was asking people to act on a claim the app had not shown yet.

options I weighed

Ask first, explain below
My first version. Quick to act on, but it asks for trust before it earns it.
Act on its own
Shut off water without asking. Fast, but a false alarm at 5 AM leaves the family with no water.
Evidence first, then two choices
Chosen. Slower by one glance, and every answer teaches Smart.

decision

If Smart can’t explain why, it doesn’t act. It asks.

The chart of expected against actual comes first, then two choices. “That was us” is a full answer: it logs the event and teaches Smart the household’s pattern. Smart is a rule engine over 30 days of the home’s own data, not a chatbot or a trained model.

trade-off

Slower by one glance

The chart takes a moment to read before the buttons. For a possible leak, a few seconds matter less than a wrong shut-off.

the mess · a part that isn’t built

I designed for a valve the kit doesn’t have

The first alert’s main button was “Close the inlet valve”. The valve is still planned hardware, so in the homes where the kit is installed that button could never work. The alert now offers what the kit can do, pause automatic filling, with an undo. Homes with a valve will get “Close the inlet valve”.

result

Not measured yet

Every answer is logged, so a study can count how often households agree with what Smart saw.

Decision 4 · micro · week 5

Why the tank speaks in litres, not just percent

“78%” means little at the tap. “1,560 of 2,000 litres, full in 12 minutes” answers the question people actually ask.

before

One big percentage

Early screens led with a single percentage. In my own review, every reading needed a conversion: how many buckets is that, and how long until it’s full? The Automatic card had the same gap: it said “Automatic” without saying what it would do.

options I weighed

Percent only
Compact, and what most tank apps show. But nobody fills a bucket in percent.
Litres only
Concrete, but meaningless without knowing the size of the tank.
Both, with time to full
Chosen. Percent for the shape of the tank, litres and minutes for the question at the tap.

decision

Litres and minutes at equal weight, and settings that say what they will do.

Home shows litres beside the percentage and time to full in minutes. The fill range is set on the tank itself, and the Automatic card spells out both levels.

trade-off

More numbers on Home

Three readings where there was one. I keep them in one block so they read as a single answer.

result

Nothing left to convert

On Home and Fill range every percentage sits beside litres, and every wait is in minutes. Not measured in a study yet.

Decision 5 · iteration · after the installs

How reviewing my own app found 34 problems, and why I fixed twelve

With the kit in about 100 homes, I went back through every screen against Nielsen’s ten heuristics and the Laws of UX. The polish was hiding problems.

before

A polished app with quiet contradictions

Quiet hours banned the motor at night while Smart planned a 1:40 AM fill. The tabs were icons with no labels. Stop on Home was a bare square. The leak alert offered a valve the kit doesn’t have, and nothing showed whether the reading was live.

options I weighed

Rebuild to the letter of the review
Fix all 34, including the lime colour and every number. Weeks of work, and the app would lose the look households already know.
Leave it
It works in homes today. But these contradictions are exactly what erodes trust.
Fix what breaks trust, keep the signature
Chosen. Twelve fixes, two new screens, three things kept on purpose.

decision

Fix what breaks trust. Keep what people already recognise.

Labels on every tab, a labelled Stop in one style, a live line on the reading, a cost and a cause on every run, a leak action the kit can actually do, and quiet hours that Smart never breaks.

trade-off

Three things I kept

The lime running card, Stop inside it, and the existing numbers. Lime now means “the motor”, not “press this”, and the numbers still don’t fully reconcile across screens. Both are on my list.

Before and after: the original screens, then the rebuilt ones.

And two states the app never had: when the sensor goes quiet, and when the internet drops.

result

34 issues found, 4 of them major. 12 fixes built.

The two new screens answer the fear behind the whole product: automatic things break when nobody’s watching. Not tested with households yet.
